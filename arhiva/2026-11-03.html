Study argues anxiety and depression are better mapped as symptom networks than as DSM categories

A network analysis of 33,675 psychotherapy patients found substantial overlap between anxiety and depression subtypes, with different symptom configurations tracking different treatment-response trajectories. The authors argue for shifting from the DSM-5's categorical diagnostic labels toward symptom-level networks to better predict who benefits from which intervention, while noting no firm consensus on the subtypes has yet emerged.

Children the ~6-year-old brain (Andrei, b. 2020) and the year ahead

What is developing at age six — and what to watch for

Around age six the brain shows rapid synaptic growth and myelination in the prefrontal cortex, driving gains in executive function — attention, working memory and impulse control. Magical thinking fades as logical cause-and-effect reasoning, an early sense of time and number (simple addition/subtraction) and a stronger theory of mind emerge, though the child is still largely self-focused and needs adult help regulating frustration.

Milestones to expect over the coming year (ranges, not deadlines): cognitive — follows multi-step instructions, counts past 10, begins reading simple words; language — mostly grammatical sentences, tells stories, understood by strangers; social/emotional — deeper friendships, better (not perfect) emotional control, some persisting fears and rigid right/wrong thinking; motor — catches a ball, hops/skips, writes own name, uses scissors. Worth a closer look if there is persistent trouble following 2–3 step directions, limited vocabulary or speech hard to understand, intense outbursts that don't settle over weeks, extreme separation anxiety, or little interest in peers.

Educating a child of Andrei's age ~6, b. 2020

What the evidence supports for a six-year-old

The best-supported practices at this age are: prioritizing play-based and guided-play learning over flashcards and drill apps (decades of cognitive-science work link play to language, executive-function and social gains); daily shared book reading, which correlates strongly with language and academic skills; limiting and co-viewing screen time with high-quality content plus daily screen-free one-on-one play; and supporting self-regulation through small-group play and modeling emotion language. Much screen-time evidence derives from passive TV and may not fully generalize to interactive media.

Large study links tea drinking to lower cardiovascular risk — temperature matters

An observational study of more than 485,000 Chinese adults found that drinking tea at least three days a week was associated with roughly 11% lower vascular-disease risk, with black and moderate-strength tea most consistently protective. Drinking tea "burning hot," however, was linked to about 16% higher risk. Being observational, it cannot prove cause and effect.
