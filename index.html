Children — what develops in a ~6-year-old (Andrei, b. 2020)

The 6-year-old brain: rapid gains in language, reading and focus

Around age six, a typical child speaks in complete 5–7 word sentences, starts reading age-appropriate books and sounding out unfamiliar words, and can sustain focus on a school task for roughly 15 minutes; problem-solving, memory, attention and the ability to follow multi-step instructions all strengthen. Socially and physically, cooperative play with 2–3 peers for 20+ minutes, catching a small ball and tying shoelaces come online, though help with tricky buttons or laces is still normal.

Watch for in the coming year: persistent trouble reading or sounding out words, not meeting general language/cognitive milestones, or marked struggle in specific school subjects — worth raising with the pediatrician. These are guideposts, not strict cutoffs; raising concerns early helps ensure timely support.

How to educate a child Andrei's age (~6)

Prioritise play, including guided play, alongside structured learning

The AAP's clinical report on play urges parents and teachers to make play central: it builds planning, organisation, emotional regulation and social skills as well as language and math, and helps children cope with stress. For ages ~4–6, let the child lead and follow their curiosity, and pair reading and worksheets with recess and playful approaches rather than replacing play. Research distinguishes free play from guided play — free exploration paired with clear learning goals — and guided play is linked to stronger outcomes in STEM, literacy and social skills.

New discoveries & firsts

Blood test detects advanced CTE in living people

A JAMA study found the blood tau fragment eMTBR-tau243 is elevated in chronic traumatic encephalopathy (CTE), distinguishing advanced CTE from healthy controls with high accuracy against 11 autopsy-confirmed cases. It cannot yet detect early-stage CTE or, alone, separate CTE from Alzheimer's (the protein rises in both), and needs larger validation — but CTE is currently diagnosable only after death, so this is a notable step toward living diagnosis. Led by Washington University (St. Louis) and UCSF.
